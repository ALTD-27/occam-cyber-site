How to choose a managed detection and response provider

Managed detection and response (MDR) means someone else watches your environment and acts when something goes wrong. It is one of the more important security purchases you will make, because you are handing over the first look at your own systems.

Why the choice matters

ASD's Annual Cyber Threat Report 2024-25 says it received over 84,700 cybercrime reports, about one every six minutes. Among businesses, email compromise and business email compromise fraud were the most commonly reported types, and the report says the average self-reported cost per report for medium-sized businesses rose by 55 percent. The figures are self-reported, so the real picture is likely larger.

Detection only helps if someone reads it and acts. That is the job you are buying.

Seven questions to ask

  1. What exactly do you watch? A good answer names the tools and data sources in scope, and what is out of scope, in writing.
  2. Who is accountable for a decision, by name? A good answer is a person, not a team alias or a ticket queue.
  3. What can you do without asking me? A good answer is a list of limits agreed in writing before anything acts, with everything else waiting for approval.
  4. When are people available? A good answer states the hours of human cover plainly, and what happens outside them. Be wary of vague promises of constant cover.
  5. Where is my data held? A good answer names every tool and model that touches it, and where each one holds data, before you sign.
  6. What evidence do I get? A good answer is, for each decision: what was considered, what was ruled out, who decided, and what happened.
  7. How do I leave? A good answer is that you keep ownership of your tenant and your logs, and that access is removed cleanly when the work ends.

Red flags

  • A price that grows with the number of alerts or the volume of data, with no ceiling you can see.
  • Reports that count alerts closed but say nothing about decisions made.
  • A demonstration built on slides rather than an investigation you can follow step by step.
  • Reluctance to say what the service cannot do.
