Threat intelligence for mid-sized organisations

Threat intelligence is knowledge about who is attacking, how they get in, and what they want. Most of it is written for large security teams. Here is what a mid-sized Australian organisation can actually use.

The test: does it change a decision?

Intelligence is only useful if it changes what someone does. A list of ten thousand suspicious addresses changes nothing if nobody has the time to check them against your systems. A single warning that a group is targeting organisations like yours, with a clear action attached, can change a week.

Where to start

  • What is already built into your tools. Platforms such as Microsoft Defender and CrowdStrike Falcon use threat intelligence to decide what to flag. Find out which of it your licences include, and make sure it is switched on.
  • National advisories. ASD's Australian Cyber Security Centre publishes alerts and advisories at cyber.gov.au. They are written for Australian organisations and they are free.
  • Your sector and your suppliers. What attackers are doing to organisations like yours, and to the suppliers you depend on, tells you more than a global headline.

What to ignore

  • Long indicator lists that nobody will read.
  • Dashboards that show everything and tell you nothing about you.
  • Alerts about threats to technology you do not run.

A simple routine

  1. Know what you own. A current list of systems, accounts and suppliers is the base for everything else.
  2. Match intelligence to your own exposure. Ask of each warning: do we run this, and is it reachable from the internet?
  3. Decide who acts. Name a person for each kind of warning, so that something found on a Friday afternoon is not left until Monday.
