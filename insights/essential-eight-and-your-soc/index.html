The Essential Eight and your SOC: where they meet

The Essential Eight reduces how often attackers get in. A security operations centre, or SOC, deals with what happens when one does. You need both, and each makes the other work better.

What the Essential Eight is

The Essential Eight is a set of eight mitigation strategies published by the Australian Signals Directorate (ASD): application control, patching applications, configuring Microsoft Office macro settings, user application hardening, restricting administrative privileges, patching operating systems, multi-factor authentication, and regular backups.

ASD's maturity model grades how well each one is implemented, from level zero to level three. Many Australian Government entities are required to implement it. For most businesses it is voluntary, but customers, insurers and boards often ask about it. ASD updates the model from time to time, so check the Essential Eight page on cyber.gov.au for the current version.

What it does not do

The Essential Eight is about making compromise harder. It does not watch your environment. Even at a high maturity level, something will eventually get through: a stolen password, a user who approves a prompt they should not have, a flaw nobody has patched yet.

Where a SOC comes in

Someone has to notice when a control is bypassed or when something unusual happens, work out whether it matters, and act. That is detection and response. It is also what turns a set of controls into evidence you can show: what was seen, what was decided, and what was done.

Signals your controls already produce

  • Multi-factor authentication produces sign-in events that show unusual locations, repeated prompts and approvals nobody expected.
  • Restricted administrative privileges produce a clear record of when elevated access is used.
  • Application control produces blocks that can show an attempt to run something unfamiliar.
  • Patching and backup tools show gaps that are worth treating as risks, not just chores.

Using both well

  1. Be honest about where you are. Check each strategy against what is actually in place, not what was planned.
  2. Fix what attackers use first. Multi-factor authentication and patching close the doors that are tried most.
  3. Make sure someone reads what the controls say. A block or a failed sign-in is only useful if it reaches a person who can act.
  4. Keep the evidence. Decisions and outcomes recorded as they happen are far easier to hand to an auditor than a reconstruction.
