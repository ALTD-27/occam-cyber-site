Ransomware payment reporting in Australia: what to prepare

Under the Cyber Security Act 2024, many Australian businesses must now report a ransomware or cyber extortion payment. The time allowed is short, so the sensible moment to prepare is before anything happens.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the Act, the official guidance on cyber.gov.au and your own position with a lawyer before you rely on it.

What the rule says

  • The reporting obligation began on 30 May 2025.
  • It applies to businesses with annual turnover above a threshold of 3 million dollars, and to operators of critical infrastructure.
  • A report is due within 72 hours of making a payment, or of becoming aware that one was made on your behalf. That includes payments made for you by others, such as an insurer or an incident response firm.
  • Reports are made through a portal on cyber.gov.au.
  • The Department of Home Affairs took an education-first approach at the start, and moved to active compliance and enforcement from 1 January 2026.

What it does not change

Reporting a payment is separate from deciding whether to make one. The Australian Government does not encourage paying a ransom, and paying does not guarantee you get your data back or that it will not be sold anyway. The rule simply means a payment, if one is made, cannot stay quiet.

What to settle before an incident

  1. Who can approve a payment, and who cannot. Decide this in calm conditions, not during a crisis.
  2. Who files the report. Name a person and a back-up, with the details they will need.
  3. What your insurer and responders will do. If someone else may pay on your behalf, know how you will be told so the 72 hours are not lost.
  4. Where your records are kept. You will need a clear timeline, so keep logs and decisions somewhere that survives an incident.
  5. How you would learn something is wrong at all. The best outcome is usually to find an attack early, before it reaches this point.
